Metric artifact, not magic

The Mirage Effect in Large Language Models

Some “emergent abilities” in large language models (LLMs) can be mirages: the underlying model improves smoothly with scale, but a hard scoring rule hides that progress until it suddenly crosses a threshold.

1smooth latent capability curve
1discontinuous or thresholded metric
1fake-looking phase transition

The whole trick is the measurement rule.

The mirage effect is the gap between the curve the model is actually climbing and the curve your benchmark chooses to show you.

What improves smoothly

The model’s probability of the right answer, calibration, log loss, partial credit, or intermediate reasoning quality can improve gradually as scale increases.

What jumps abruptly

Exact match, multiple-choice pass/fail, or “all steps correct” scores can stay flat, then jump when the smooth variable crosses a cutoff.

What fools us

The plot looks like a new ability appeared at one model size, even though a smoother metric would have shown steady progress all along.

A mirage is made by thresholding a smooth signal.

The causal story is not “nothing, nothing, nothing, intelligence.” It is “small gains, hidden gains, visible gains.”

The equation is just a step function.

If the score is binary, the reported ability can jump even when the underlying ability does not.

Let \(N\) be model scale, \(s(N)\) be the model’s smooth latent skill at that scale, and \(\tau\) be the threshold a benchmark item requires for success.

\[ \boxed{A(N)=\mathbf{1}\{s(N)>\tau\}} \]

Here \(A(N)\) is the reported binary accuracy for that item. It is 0 below the threshold and 1 above it, so it contains a jump by definition.

Across many items, the same problem becomes a staircase.

With items \(i=1,\ldots,m\), each with its own threshold \(\tau_i\), reported accuracy is:

\[ \boxed{\operatorname{Acc}(N)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\{s_i(N)>\tau_i\}} \]

If many thresholds sit near the same model scale, the staircase can look like a phase transition.

The diagnostic is simple: change the score.

A real scientific claim about emergence should survive reasonable changes in measurement.

Do this before claiming emergence

  • Plot log loss, probability of the correct answer, or partial credit alongside exact match.
  • Check whether the “critical scale” moves when the threshold changes.
  • Show per-item curves, not only the aggregate benchmark score.
  • Report uncertainty when there are few examples near the transition.

Do not confuse these phenomena

  • A hallucination is a false generated claim.
  • Prompt brittleness is sensitivity to wording.
  • The mirage effect is a measurement artifact: a metric makes gradual change look sudden.

Bottom line: emergence may be real in some cases, but this plot shape alone does not prove it.

The mirage effect says to inspect the continuous variables underneath a thresholded score before telling a story about sudden new abilities.
